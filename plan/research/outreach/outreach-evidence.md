## Ten evidence-backed principles

**1. You will under-ask.** Flynn & Lake (Bohns) 2008, *JPSP*, 6 studies: help-seekers underestimated compliance by up to ~50%; they weigh the helper's cost of "yes" and ignore the social cost of "no". `[peer-reviewed]` https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/if-you-need-help-just-ask-underestimating-compliance-direct-requests
→ Double whatever number of asks feels right — for synchronous asks (see #2).

**2. Email is the one channel where you *over*-estimate compliance.** Roghanizad & Bohns 2017, *JESP*: 45 requesters × 10 strangers each; face-to-face ~34× more effective than identical cold email; both groups predicted ~50% success; targets read stranger email "with suspicion". `[peer-reviewed]` https://ecommons.cornell.edu/handle/1813/75289 . Roghanizad & Bohns 2022, *SPPS*: 490 requesters, 1,490 targets: in-person > video ≈ phone > email/text. `[peer-reviewed]` https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2021/12/best-results-ask-person-instead-over-zoom
→ Written outreach requests a 15-min call; the real ask happens live.

**3. Asking for advice raises how competent you look — most when the problem is hard and the advisor is an expert.** Brooks, Gino & Schweitzer 2015, *Management Science*, 5+ experiments (n≈114–302 each). `[peer-reviewed]` https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Advice%20Seeking_59ad2c42-54d6-4b32-8517-a99eeae0a45c.pdf
→ First contact = "I'd value your advice on X specific hard problem", not "would you like to collaborate".

**4. Forecasts about strangers are pessimistic, and practice recalibrates them within a week.** Epley & Schroeder 2014 (9 experiments): talking to strangers rated more positive than forecast. Boothby et al. 2018 "liking gap" (5 studies): people underestimate how much partners liked them; gap larger for shyer people. Sandstrom, Boothby & Cooney 2022: a week-long talk-to-strangers exercise (N=198) reduced fear of rejection; expectations grew more accurate daily. `[peer-reviewed]` https://guscooney.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Talking-to-Strangers.pdf
→ Outreach as a daily rep (one message/day for two weeks), not a batch. Silence is not dislike.

**5. Opportunities travel through weak and *moderately* weak ties.** Granovetter 1973/74 (jobs: 27.8% via contacts seen rarely, 55.6% occasionally). Rajkumar et al. 2022, *Science*: randomised experiments on >20M LinkedIn users; inverted-U — ~10 mutual connections transmit most jobs; weak ties matter most in digital industries. `[peer-reviewed]` https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/a-causal-test-of-the-strength-of-weak-ties
→ Prioritise second-degree contacts with a few mutual connections over both best friends and total strangers.

**6. Reconnecting dormant ties is the cheapest high-yield move.** Levin, Walter & Murnighan 2011, *Org. Science*: 224 executives asked dormant contacts for advice on a live project; reconnected formerly-strong ties gave novelty *and* trust. `[peer-reviewed]` https://business.gwu.edu/sites/g/files/zaxdzs5326/files/15_FP.SP_Walter.J_15levin_2011a.pdf
→ Before any cold email, list 10 people you liked and lost touch with 3+ years ago; ask each for advice by phone/video.

**7. Brokerage across groups that don't talk is where ideas get judged valuable.** Burt 2004, *AJS*: managers spanning structural holes more likely to voice ideas and have them rated valuable. Guimerà/Uzzi 2005, *Science*: teams mixing newcomers and incumbents outperform. `[peer-reviewed]` https://snap.stanford.edu/class/cs224w-readings/Burt04StructureHole.pdf
→ The pitch is the bridge itself: "I sit in three of these worlds; here is what each doesn't know about the others."

**8. Warm paths dominate for money; cold inbound is ~10% of VC deal flow.** Gompers, Gornall, Kaplan & Strebulaev (885 VCs): ~30% own network, 20% other investors, ~10% inbound; 200 screened per 4 funded. `[peer-reviewed survey]` https://www.nber.org/papers/w22587 . Bol, de Vaan & van de Rijt 2018, *PNAS*: grant near-winners accumulated >2× the funding of near-misses over 8 years; a key mechanism is near-misses *stop applying*. `[peer-reviewed]` https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2018/04/180427100301.htm
→ Work backwards from a person who can introduce you; treat rejection as a signal to reapply.

**9. Short, specific cold email to busy experts works.** Milkman, Akinola & Chugh 2015 audit: 6,548 professors at 259 US universities, identical brief request to "briefly talk about your work"; **67% replied** (with large documented bias by sender name/ethnicity). `[peer-reviewed]` https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~dchugh/articles/SSRN22Apr.pdf
→ A 100-word single-ask email about *their* work is not a long shot.

**10. Follow up 2–3 times; most replies come from follow-ups.** Backlinko/Pitchbox 12M emails: 8.5% overall reply; one follow-up +65.8%; 2–4 contacts at one org +93%. Woodpecker 20M+: 9%→13% with ≥1 follow-up; 2–3 optimal, 2–3+ days apart. `[vendor-benchmark]` https://backlinko.com/email-outreach-study · https://woodpecker.co/blog/follow-up-statistics/
→ Pre-write follow-ups 1 and 2 (day +4, day +11) before sending message 1; stop at 3.

## Norms by audience

**Academics (HCI / low vision)** — read 1–2 recent papers; open with a specific question about their work; individual emails only; follow up ~1 week; say who you already work with and whether co-authorship is on the table; grad students/postdocs are lower-friction first contacts. `[factual-source]` https://pcur.princeton.edu/2017/11/advice-on-reaching-out-to-faculty . Ethics: HRECs may decline unaffiliated applicants; the practical route to interviewing patients *as research* is a university/hospital partner sponsoring the HREC application. Informal customer-discovery conversations are not "research" — don't present them as such. `[factual-source]` https://devpolicy.org/improving-the-ethics-approval-process-for-non-university-researchers-20190218/

**Clinicians** — top barriers: no protected time, never hearing what happened to the research. Facilitators: researchers who sell benefit to patients, respect clinical pressure, show up consistently, own the relationship-maintenance (UK, 4 focus groups, n=18). `[peer-reviewed, qual]` https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7718701/ → ask 15 min, offer to demo in clinic, promise and deliver a one-paragraph outcome note. "Does this simulation match what you see clinically?" is a question only they can answer. `[my-synthesis]`

**Charities** — Australian care/disability service leaders (21 interviews, 2024–25) want co-designed, implementation-ready research, equitable long-term partnerships, and recognition their staff are "drained"; funding not interest is the binding constraint. `[peer-reviewed, qual]` https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12682803/ → lead with what you give. No survey data on how charities triage unsolicited proposals. Vision Australia research-listing requirements: verify by phone 1300 847 466.

**RP peers** — "nothing about us without us": meaning-shaping role, not subjects. Pay people: Safer Care Victoria guide — sessional, up to ~$237/day member `[factual-source]` https://www.safercare.vic.gov.au/sites/default/files/2019-01/A%20guide%20to%20consumer%20remuneration.pdf ; HCNSW July 2025 tiers $43.50–$64.50/hr → **budget ~$50–65/hr for a 45–60 min interview.** Recruitment works when "relationship-forward" rather than transactional (37 participatory-AI projects). `[peer-reviewed, small]` https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.20176 . Lived-experience positioning raising response: plausible, untested. `[my-synthesis]`

**Funders** — warm path first (#8); ask the officer for advice on fit before applying (#3); reapply after a near miss (#8). Retina Australia grants target "the best researchers in Australia" → academic partner as applicant.

**Potential technical co-founders** — look among moderately weak and dormant ties (#5, #6); prefer a newcomer to your circle (#7). Run discovery, not a pitch: Blank (founders do the interviews themselves, don't pitch, ~10–15 conversations/week) and Fitzpatrick's Mom Test (their life not your idea; specifics in the past; talk less) — `[practitioner, not evidence]`. "Build in public" as recruiting: no peer-reviewed evaluation. `[my-synthesis]`

## What the evidence does NOT support

- "Email is safe and efficient for first asks." It is the channel where you overestimate your persuasiveness.
- "Asking for advice makes me look like I don't know what I'm doing." Opposite, for hard problems asked of experts.
- "Ask my close network." Close ties are informationally redundant.
- "Don't bother old contacts out of the blue." Dormant ties beat current ties.
- "Long, thorough emails show respect." Every dataset says the reverse for openers.
- "Send Tuesday 10am." ~2-point effect; noise.
- "One well-crafted email is enough." Most replies come after follow-ups.
- Benjamin Franklin effect as a tactic: real 1969 study, small, unreplicated modern form. Don't build on it.
- "Include ROI / impact statements." Pitch language reduced meetings booked (vendor data; consistent with the suspicion mechanism).

## Open questions the literature can't answer

1. Does disclosing lived experience raise cold-outreach response? No controlled study.
2. How do Australian eye charities triage unsolicited proposals? No data.
3. Do B2B email magnitudes transfer to academics/clinicians? Only the Milkman audit is directly relevant.
4. Optimal follow-up cadence for academics — the "~1 week" rule is folklore.
5. Does mentioning a mutual contact causally raise reply rate? No RCT.
6. Video vs phone for cold strangers — the 2022 study used friends.

**Confidence:** high on #1, #2, #3, #5, #6; medium on #8, #9; low on all vendor magnitudes.

**Cheapest independent check of the whole approach:** two-arm test — 10 cold emails asking for a 15-min *advice* call vs 10 asking to "collaborate/partner", each with two pre-written follow-ups; count replies at day 14. If the advice arm doesn't beat the partner arm, the framing advice is wrong for your audience.
